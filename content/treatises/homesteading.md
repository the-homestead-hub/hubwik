# Homesteading

Homestead is older than the American Act that borrowed it. Old English hāmstede named a home-place: house, land, and the work that kept them. The Homestead Hub uses that older sense — a working place — not a 160-acre claim on the public domain.

## The word

<p>Old English <em>hāmstede</em> is <em>hām</em> (home, dwelling, estate) plus <em>stede</em> (place, stead). Etymonline glosses it as a family's dwelling place, town, or village. Wiktionary traces Middle English <em>hamstede</em> / <em>hemstede</em> in place-names from Proto-West Germanic <em>*haimastadi</em>. The same formation gives Dutch <em>heemstede</em>, German <em>Heimstätte</em>, and the English place-name Hampstead — a doublet, not a descendant of the American statute.</p>
<p>In Britain the ordinary sense stayed close to that: a house with its land and outbuildings, especially on a farm. George Eliot can write of “large brick-and-stone homesteads” in <em>Silas Marner</em> (1861) without meaning a federal land grant.</p>

## The 1862 Act

<p>The United States Homestead Act (20 May 1862) defined a homestead, for that statute, as 160 acres of public-domain land, claimed by living on it and improving it for five years. The verb <em>to homestead</em> and the agent <em>homesteader</em> in American English are from that practice (1860s). The Act is a later, local meaning. It did not coin the word, and it is not the origin of a British shop named The Homestead Hub.</p>
<p>Using “homesteading” as if it started in 1862 is a category error. The American quarter-section is one chapter. The older home-place is the one this book is in.</p>

## What the Hub means

<p>The Homestead Hub is a working place that sells the means to grow food at home: open seed, peat-free coco, a stack whose planter is the pot, a lamp sized for that stack. Homesteading here is the practice of keeping a home-place productive in the space you have — a sill, a courtyard, a kit — not a reenactment of westward settlement.</p>
<p>Food re-sovereignty — growing a plate you would otherwise buy — is written on the kit treatises, where the numbers live: <a href="/pages/three-by-four">Three by Four</a> and <a href="/pages/four-by-four">Four by Four</a>. It is not the hall's headline. The lore page is the etymology and the distinction.</p>
