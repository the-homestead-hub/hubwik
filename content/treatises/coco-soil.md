# Coco Soil

Coco Soil is a peat-free growing medium: 70% coco coir and 30% perlite, pre-buffered with calcium and magnesium, with Ecothrive Charge. One five-litre bag fills one Homestead Hub planter tier to its lip. £6.99 a bag; four bags £19.99 on the same page — a price rule, not a second product.

## The bag

<p>Live on the shop as <a href="/products/coco-soil">Coco Soil</a>. Packed by The Homestead Hub. The copy on the bag is the blend, the fill, and the name. One bag, one tier. The four-bag price is a page rule on that URL, not a second SKU.</p>
<p>Copy may say growing medium. Do not read “soil” here as peat-based garden soil or as a mineral topsoil. It is coco and perlite.</p>

## Why coco

<p>Coco coir is the dust and short fibre of the coconut husk. It is a by-product of the coconut industry, not a crop grown for compost. It holds water, re-wets after drying, and keeps a more stable structure than a peat that collapses as it decomposes. Perlite is the aeration: 30% of this bag, so the 70% coir does not sit waterlogged in a 5L planter.</p>
<p>The Homestead Hub did not invent coco. It chose a peat-free 70:30 and a named charge (Ecothrive) so a kit planter can be filled from one bag without a second trip into a peat bog.</p>

## Versus peat

<p>Peatlands store carbon. Draining them for extraction releases it. The Royal Horticultural Society's peat-free advice is the ordinary UK horticultural position: peat-free compost is widely available and more sustainable than peat-based compost; going peat-free conserves bog habitat and cuts that carbon release. Coir is one of the common peat-free ingredients, with wood fibre and others. Coir does not hold nutrients as well as some mixes; that is why this bag is pre-buffered and charged, and why a separate Growth Formula exists on the shop.</p>
<p>THH has not published a house laboratory comparison of this bag against a named peat compost. The claim on this page is the blend, the fill, the peat-free fact, and the public horticultural case against peat extraction — not a THH yield trial.</p>

## Versus other media

<p>Rockwool is inert, high porosity, and not a soil story. Peat–vermiculite mixes were the old greenhouse default. Garden soil in a 5L planter slumps and muddies. A 2017 tomato-substrate paper in <em>Frontiers in Plant Science</em> found coconut coir increased fruit yield against rockwool and against peat–vermiculite in that trial; it is one paper, on tomato, not a certificate for basil on a sill. It is here so a reader who wants a study has one, not so the shop can pretend the paper was ours.</p>

## How to fill a planter

<ol>
<li>Open one 5L bag per Homestead Hub planter tier.</li>
<li>Fill to the lip. Do not pack it like earth.</li>
<li>Water until it runs. Coco re-wets; do not be frightened by the first dry litre.</li>
<li>Sow per the crop treatise. Micros on the surface. Herbs about 1 cm. Roots about 2 cm.</li>
</ol>
